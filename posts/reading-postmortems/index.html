Why postmortems are good reading

A well-written postmortem is one of the few places in engineering where you get an honest, detailed account of exactly how something went wrong, with none of the marketing gloss applied to product launches. Reading them is cheap access to expensive lessons someone else already paid for.

What a good one contains

  • A precise timeline — timestamps, not vague phases.
  • The actual trigger, separated from the conditions that let it cascade.
  • What made detection slow or fast, in concrete terms.
  • Follow-up actions with owners, not just "we will improve monitoring."

Patterns across dozens of postmortems

After enough of these, the specifics blur together but the shapes repeat:

  1. The root cause is rarely one thing — it's a change plus a monitoring gap plus a runbook nobody had rehearsed.
  2. The fix that ships fastest is often a manual mitigation, and the real fix — the one that prevents a repeat — lands weeks later as a follow-up item.
  3. Alerting on symptoms (error rate, latency) catches more real incidents than alerting on causes (CPU, disk) ever does.

Building your own reading habit

I keep a running list of public postmortems, sorted loosely by what they're good examples of: detection gaps, cascading failures, deploy-related regressions, and "the runbook was wrong" incidents. Revisiting the list before designing a new system is a quick way to check whether you're about to reintroduce a failure mode someone else already wrote up in detail.
